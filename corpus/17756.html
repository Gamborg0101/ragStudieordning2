1. About the degree programme

1.1 The academic direction and primary subject areas of the programme

The goal of the Bachelor’s Degree Programme in English is to fulfil requirements at the Bachelor’s level for qualifying for teaching at upper secondary schools (Danish gymnasier) and in vocational education as well as prepare students for careers within the private, public and not-for-profit sectors. To those ends, the Bachelor's Degree Programme combines the study of language, communication, culture, media, literature, history and society.

The obligatory courses and electives of the Bachelor's Degree Programme are organised in the disciplinary areas English linguistics, literature in English, and the history, society and culture of the English-speaking world.

Within English linguistics, students study the English language at a high academic level, working with the levels of language (phonetics, phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics and pragmatics) as well as variation and change within the English language. This area of study is covered primarily by the core courses English Linguistics 1: Levels of Language; English Linguistics 2: Structure and Change; and English Linguistics 3: English in its Social Settings. 

Within literature in English, students work with both the forms and genres of literature, drawing on a range of literary texts from the English-speaking world, a historical overview of literature in English, from Old English literature to contemporary literature, as well as British, American, postcolonial, Commonwealth and other English-language literary texts that represent distinct traditions and contemporary literary, social, theoretical and cultural concerns. This area of study is covered primarily by the core courses Literature in English 1: Form and Genre; Literature in English 2: Literature in Time; and Literature in English 3: Societies, Texts and Identities. 

Within the study of the history, society and culture of the English-speaking world, emphasis is placed on the historical and social conditions of the United Kingdom, Ireland, the United States and other selected parts of the Commonwealth and the English-speaking world. The study of culture includes a substantial focus on media forms, theory and products. This area of study is covered primarily by the obligatory core courses History, Society and Culture 1: The United Kingdom, Ireland and the Commonwealth; History, Society and Culture 2: American History and Society; and History, Society and Culture 3: Media and Culture. 

Students can specialise further by their choice of electives and Bachelor's project topic. A range of courses work with communication as a skill, knowledge set, and competence - for example, through academic writing and oral presentations. The Bachelor’s Degree Programme in English also introduces theory and method as a fundamental component of the study of the language, literature, history, society and culture of the English-speaking world. Finally, the Bachelor's Degree Programme includes a supplementary subject of 45 ECTS. Together with a Master's Degree Programme in English and qualifying Bachelor's and Master's supplementary subjects, the degree programme fulfils the requirements for teaching at upper secondary schools.


1.2 Learning outcomes

Students acquire the following knowledge, skills and competences during the Bachelor’s Degree Programme in English:

Knowledge:

  • Research-based knowledge of theories and methods within the study of the English language and literature in English as well as the history, society and culture of the English-speaking world
  • Analytical knowledge and understanding of how the English language works
  • Knowledge of the variations of the English language (regional, social, functional and historical)
  • Knowledge of literary texts, periods and authors of the English-speaking world
  • Knowledge of history, society and culture in the English-speaking world, including media forms, theory and products
  • Knowledge of digital tools and methods applied to the study of the humanities
  • The ability to understand, explain, reflect on and evaluate theories, academic methods and practice within the study of English.

Skills:

  • The ability to identify and analyse complex issues within the study of English
  • The ability to work in a cross-disciplinary/inter-disciplinary manner and to make use of the various academic areas of the degree programme, as well as to acquire relevant knowledge within new areas
  • The ability to work methodically, independently and critically, and to adjust the methods used to the nature of the field being dealt with
  • The ability to use information search technology to find relevant primary and secondary sources for academic assignments within the subject areas of the programme and as required by the assignment
  • The ability to use reference works, academic databases and other academic information sources
  • The ability to assess and use various types of primary, secondary and tertiary sources, both historical and contemporary, with a view to cross-disciplinary analysis and understanding
  • The ability to use digital tools and methods in the study of the humanities
  • The ability to participate in the production of a multimedia product
  • The ability to consider and assess the applicability of relevant theories and methods and to apply them correctly
  • The ability to apply academic assessment criteria in the selection of relevant information and materials for the degree programme, including the ability to distinguish between academic and popular representations, as well as between primary, secondary and tertiary sources
  • The ability to communicate information and ideas in English in a proficient and appropriate manner in speech as well as in writing
  • The ability to argue in a consistent and evidence-based manner in English in both speech and writing.

Competences:

  • Competences to address and analyse complex situations and develop solutions in an ethical and academic manner within the context of English studies
  • Competences to plan and execute projects within a given time frame
  • Competences to collaborate with others on a range of topics within the study of English
  • Competences to structure knowledge and present it in well-composed and correctly framed English in writing and in speech, including in the use of digital and other media
  • Competences to identify one’s own learning needs and to structure one’s own learning within the study of English as well as in other learning environments.

2.1.3 Forms of instruction
The programme will involve a variety of forms of teaching and student work. 
Teaching forms may include but are not limited to: lecturing, seminar instruction, leading in-class discussions, leading the close reading of set texts, asking and answering questions, assigning and clarifying tasks, providing supervision and feedback, and providing course materials and course-related information on Brightspace.

Student work will include a variety of work forms, which may include but are not limited to: preparing individually and in groups for seminars and lectures, independent study activities, completing assignments, preparing and delivering individual or group student presentations in class, asking and answering questions, participating in classroom discussions, leading the close reading of set texts, practical training (guided / structured individual and group practice in class), leading seminars and discussions individually or in groups, conducting literature searches and carrying out research, incorporating and using supervision and feedback, including giving and receiving peer feedback, interacting with individuals, organizations, and companies, completing midterm and end-of-term course evaluations, and preparing for the exam.

Other teaching and student work forms may include teaching by student instructors, workshops, online interaction and tasks, field trips and excursions, and guest lecturers.
Forms of both teaching and student work will be appropriate for the course purpose and aligned with the learning outcomes and exam. The forms of teaching and student work for a given course will be announced on Brightspace and in course guides at the beginning of the semester. Forms of teaching and student work may be subject to change.

Description of qualifications:

Purpose:
The objective of the course is to familiarise the student with the phonetics, phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics and pragmatics of the English language.

The students are introduced to the description and analysis of the phonetic properties of the sounds of English and of the sound systems of the main variants of English. The students are also introduced to the description and analysis of English word structure, of English sentence structure, and of the meaning of words and sentences in English.

The knowledge, skills and competences acquired in this course form part of the basis for English Linguistics 2: Structure and Change and English Linguistics 3: English in its Social Settings. Both the ability to apply the basic tools of linguistic description and analysis to the English language and the ability to assess critically the student's own English language abilities and that of others are essential to study the English language in the following semesters.

Academic objectives:
In the evaluation of the student’s performance, emphasis is placed on the extent to which the student is able to:

Knowledge:
- accurately describe and discuss concepts within and approaches to the study of the phonetics, phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics and pragmatics of the English language
- apply linguistic theory and methods when describing and discussing concepts within and approaches to the study of the phonetics, phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics and pragmatics of the English language.

Skills:
- accurately describe, analyse and discuss examples of spoken and written English from the perspective of phonetics, phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics and pragmatics using the relevant linguistic terminology and methods
- correctly transcribe speech in varieties of English using the International Phonetic Alphabet
- communicate in written English about the chosen topic in a coherent, well-argued and meaningful way adhering to academic writing standards for grammar, spelling and style, using appropriate concepts and terms.

Competences:
- plan and carry out academic assignments ethically, responsibly and independently within a given time frame.

History, Society and Culture 1: The United Kingdom, Ireland and the Commonwealth 10 ECTS Semester 1 7-point grading scale
1st year exam
Exam details See courses

Description of qualifications:

Purpose:
The objective of this course is to expand the student’s knowledge of the history, society and culture of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland (UK), the Republic of Ireland, as well as of selected aspects of British imperial history and the contemporary Commonwealth, with a particular focus on Canada. Drawing on primary and secondary sources, both historical and contemporary, and through the application of relevant concepts, theories and analytical methods, the course aims to improve the student’s ability to assess, explain and interpret various political, economic, social and cultural processes in the development of the UK and Ireland as well as in relation to selected Commonwealth countries. The goal is furthermore to enable students to produce written work on topics within the course’s subject areas. The course also seeks to develop the student’s capacity to independently apply the knowledge and skills developed in this course elsewhere in the study program.

The course explores major themes, conflicts, processes and transformations in the history, society and culture of the UK, Ireland and the Commonwealth, from circa 400 CE to the present day. It outlines complex historical continuities and changes in various thematic areas which can include: the early settling and shaping of the British Isles; processes of political unification and de-unification; the Reformation and religious conflicts; the evolution from absolutist to constitutional forms of governance; empire-building, decolonisation and post-imperial legacies; the impact of Brexit on the UK and its constituent parts (England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland); industrialisation and competing economic and social visions; and tensions between cultural and national identities in the UK. The course furthermore addresses topics such as: feudalism, absolutism and constitutionalism; nationalism, national identity and Britishness; imperialism, colonialism, decolonisation and post-colonialism; slavery, gender and race; immigration, identity and multiculturalism, and; the UK’s historical, contemporary and future role in the world.

The knowledge, skills and competences cultivated in this course provides foundations upon which students are equipped to take History, Society and Culture 2: American History and Society as well as History, Society and Culture 3: Media and Culture and subsequent electives (including the Bachelor's project) on the Bachelor’s Degree Programme in English, as well as core courses and electives on the Master’s Degree Programme in English. The course also provides a context for Literature in English 2: Literature in Time; Literature in English 3: Societies, Texts and Identities; and English linguistics 3: English in its Social Settings.

Academic objectives:
In the evaluation of the student’s performance, emphasis is placed on the extent to which the student is able to:

Knowledge:
- accurately identify, illustrate and describe specified aspects of UK, Irish, and Commonwealth history, politics, social, and cultural conditions and institutions, and/or of the development of the British Empire and the Commonwealth
- accurately and critically explain and interpret specified events, developments, patterns, relationships and/or processes, historical and/or contemporary.

Skills:                                                             
- locate and incorporate relevant primary and secondary sources as required by the assignment, using digital technology where relevant, and apply academic assessment criteria in the selection of relevant information and materials, including distinguishing between academic and popular as well as between primary, secondary and tertiary sources
- skilfully and critically assess and use various types of primary and secondary sources, both historical and/or contemporary

- communicate in written English about the exam topic in a coherent, well-argued and meaningful way adhering to academic writing standards for grammar, spelling and style, and following academic conventions of layout, notes, referencing and quotations in accordance with the Department of English Style Guide.

1st year exam
Exam details See courses

Description of qualifications:

Purpose:
The objective of the course is to provide the student with (A) knowledge of major aspects of literature, literary genres, and literary analysis, with special reference to literature in English; (B) the ability to apply this knowledge to the oral and written analysis and discussion of specific literary texts; and (C) competence in critical thinking and argumentation in a wide range of contexts.

The course exposes the student to a broad selection of British, American and postcolonial texts from various periods, so as to introduce major literary genres, including a play by Shakespeare, poetry, prose fiction and plays, sub-genres such the novel, the sonnet, tragedy and comedy, as well as fundamental aspects of literary form, style and agency, such a metre, irony, narration and the author. In addition, teaching focuses on principles and methods of literary analysis and on developing evidence-based arguments about literary texts through critical academic writing, including the critical use of secondary texts such as monographs and articles concerning the primary texts in question.

The course provides the student with the basic skills and knowledge necessary to understand and profit from more advanced and specialised literature courses, especially Literature in English 2: Literature in Time, as well as contributing to the degree programme’s introduction to English Studies as a composite discipline, especially with regard to connections between literary analysis and the broader study of language. The course equips the student with the most basic knowledge and skills necessary for future employment in teaching and other professional fields that involve textual analysis, working with creative writing, and/or knowledge and understanding of English-language culture.

Academic objectives:
In the evaluation of the student’s performance, emphasis is placed on the extent to which the student is able to:

Knowledge:
- accurately identify, illustrate and describe general aspects of literary studies, significant literary genres and formal literary analysis
- accurately and critically discuss specific texts from the British, American and postcolonial literary traditions
- select, describe, explain and apply relevant theory (or theories) from the study of literature in English, identifying, articulating and properly using methods, concepts and terms used within the theory and practice of the analysis of literary form and genre.

Skills:
- skilfully conduct literary analysis, with special reference to literature in English and aspects of form and genre
- interpret, argue cogently and soundly, and/or critically evaluate as required by the assignment or topic and consistent with the applied method and theory, with a clear thesis and relevant claims, supported by relevant, sufficient, representative, and accurate evidence compiled from a variety of critically assessed primary and secondary sources
- find and incorporate relevant primary and secondary sources as required by the assignment, using digital technology where appropriate; and apply academic assessment criteria in the selection of relevant information and materials, including distinguishing between academic and popular sources
- communicate in written English about the chosen topic in a coherent, well-argued and meaningful way, adhering to academic writing standards for grammar, spelling, punctuation and style, following academic conventions of layout, notes, referencing and quotations in accordance with the Department of English Style Guide.

Competences:
- plan and carry out academic research and writing ethically, responsibly and independently.

Description of qualifications:

Purpose:
The objective of the course is to develop the student’s knowledge of English morphology, syntax, semantics and pragmatics at an advanced level as well as their knowledge of the history of the English language.

The students are introduced to the detailed grammatical analysis of English, which includes the analysis of the constituent structure of English words and clauses. The students are also introduced to historical variants such as Old English, Middle English, Early Modern English and Late Modern English, as well as to the historical basis for the present-day social and regional variation in English.

This course builds on the knowledge, skills and competences acquired in English Linguistics 1: Levels of Language. The knowledge, skills and competences acquired in this course form part of the basis for English Linguistics 3: English in its Social Settings. The ability to describe and analyse English words, phrases, and clauses in detail, the ability to be able to relate properties of Modern English to their historical roots, and the ability to critically assess the student's own English language abilities and that of others are essential to study the English language in the following semesters.

History, Society and Culture 2: American History and Society 10 ECTS Semester 2 7-point grading scale
1st year exam
Exam details See courses

Description of qualifications:

Purpose:
The objective of this course is to expand the student’s knowledge of American history, politics and society, as well as of the nation’s ideologies, institutions and global influence. Drawing on primary, secondary and tertiary sources, past and present, print and digital, the course also aims to improve the student’s ability, through the application of relevant concepts, theories and analytical methods, to assess, explain and interpret various dimensions of the nation’s development, and to produce written work addressing topics within the course’s subject areas. It also seeks to foster the student’s capacity to independently enhance and apply elsewhere (also in collaboration with others) the knowledge and skills developed in this course.

The course explores major themes, patterns, developments and conflicts in American history, politics and society, from the pre-colonial era to the present day. It outlines phases, continuities and changes in the nation’s history, identifies key ideologies and institutions, introduces concepts, theories and analytical frameworks that have thrown light on the nation’s development, and highlights how understandings of the present-day United States call for an informed, critical knowledge of its past. The course addresses topics such as liberty and equality, individualism and community, nationalism and regionalism, self-reliance and welfare, business and labor, slavery and race, immigration and identity, ethnicity and gender, domestic reform and overseas expansion, and hot and cold wars. It also surveys the growth of the United States from its origins as a British colonial outpost to its contemporary status as global superpower.

The course builds on the knowledge, skills and competences cultivated in History, Society and Culture 1: The United Kingdom, Ireland and the Commonwealth. The historical components of the course contribute background contextual information for History, Society and Culture 3: Media and Culture; Literature in English 2: Literature in Time; and English Linguistics 2: Structure and Change.

- write a thesis statement and make relevant claims that are supported by credible, sufficient, representative, relevant, and accurate evidence synthesised from a variety of critically assessed primary and secondary sources
- analyse, interpret, argue cogently and effectively and/or critically evaluate as required by the assignment or topic and consistent with the applied method and theory

Purpose:
The objective of the course is to provide the student with (A) knowledge of major literary periods and movements within British, American and postcolonial literature in English from the Middle Ages until the present day; (B) the ability to relate this knowledge to the understanding, analysis and oral and written discussion of specific literary texts; (C) competence in critical, historically-informed argumentation in a wide range of contexts.

The course exposes the student to a broad selection of British, American and postcolonial texts, including a play by Shakespeare, poetry, prose fiction and plays from the Middle Ages to the present. Emphasis is placed on significant historical periods and movements, such as the Renaissance, the Enlightenment, Romanticism and Modernism, within an overall chronological perspective. The course develops the student’s understanding of the relationship between the narrative and thematic content of literary texts and the real-world social, political and intellectual contexts in which they are created; it evaluates historical facts as a source of insight into literature, and literature as a source of insight into history. In addition, teaching focuses on principles and methods of literary analysis and on developing evidence-based arguments about literary texts through critical academic writing, including the critical use of secondary texts such as monographs and articles concerning the primary texts in question and their historical background.

The course builds upon the basic skills and knowledge about literature and literary analysis acquired in Literature in English 1: Form and Genre, as well as preparing the student for more advanced and specialised literature courses, especially those that emphasise the study of literature within its historical context. The course also contributes to the degree programme’s presentation of English Studies as a composite discipline, especially with regard to connections between literary analysis and the broader study of society, politics, identity and communication, including concepts such as nationality, community and class. The course develops the student’s profile of knowledge and skills necessary for future employment in teaching and other professional fields that involve textual analysis, working with creative writing, and/or knowledge and understanding of English-language culture.

Description of qualifications:

Purpose:
The objective of this course is to equip students with knowledge about the intellectual background, theoretical underpinnings and methodological practices of English Studies (the study of the English language, literature in English, and the history, society and culture of the English-speaking world) specifically and the humanities more generally. It enables students to identify continuities and discontinuities within and across the different branches of English Studies and in relation to other academic disciplines. It qualifies students to discuss the current academic situation within the study of English in a broader context that includes philosophy and the history of ideas, as well as social, cultural and ethical perspectives.

The course provides an introduction to theory and practice within the humanities and specific analyses and discussions of theoretical and methodological issues relating to the study of (A) language, (B) literature and (C) history, society and culture.

The course builds upon the obligatory courses in English linguistics, literature and history, society and culture of the first two semesters, and requires students to reflect upon the ideas informing the work that they have been doing and will continue to do. By broadening the students’ intellectual horizon and deepening their disciplinary self-awareness, it increases their chance of making successful academic and career choices. By highlighting how English Studies is conceptualised in comparison with other academic disciplines, the course prepares students to engage in the type of cross-disciplinary collaborative work that is required of Danish secondary school teachers.

Description of qualifications:

Purpose:
The objective of the course is to provide the student with (A) knowledge of major issues and debates concerning the analysis and application of literature in relation to ideas of personal and collective voice and identity, especially as these are expressed in situations of social, cultural and political conflict within and between individuals and communities within an English-language context; (B) knowledge of how these issues and debates apply in a range of specific contexts, and how they are addressed and developed in a specific selection of English-language literary texts; and (C) competence in critical thinking and argumentation, relating the study of literature to a range of allied disciplines.

The course is based on the reading and analysis of significant British, American, postcolonial and/or other English-language literary texts. Emphasis is placed on texts that represent two or more distinct national or regional traditions and/or identity categories such as race, gender or class. The course addresses the role of literature in relation to identity formation, understood as giving expression to various voices, as well as in addressing, mediating and influencing social, cultural and political conflicts. Furthermore, the course provides an introduction to a range of cross-disciplinary theoretical approaches, relevant to the chosen texts and conflicts. Teaching focuses on themes of voice, identity and conflict within the selected literary texts; theoretical issues in the application of literature to real situations and events; and the development of evidence-based arguments in oral discussion and through academic writing, including the critical use of secondary texts such as monographs and articles concerning the literary texts and social, cultural and political issues in question.

The course will include some elements from English linguistics 3: English in its Social Settings and will emphasise ways in which linguistics and literary studies can reinforce each other.

The course builds upon the broad range of texts, contexts and approaches to literature that the student will have encountered in Literature in English 1: Form and Genre and Literature in English 2; Literature in Time, especially in relation to ways in which identity may be constructed through ‘persons’ such as authors, readers, speakers, narrators and characters, and to the particular characteristics of literature produced within various national, geographical, historical, cultural and political contexts. The course completes the degree programme’s survey of major aspects of, and approaches to, the study of literature in English, and thus prepares the student for more advanced courses and projects that involve the integration of several aspects and approaches, including the Bachelor's project. The course equips the student with further knowledge and skills necessary for future employment in teaching and other professional fields that involve textual analysis, working with creative writing, and/or knowledge and understanding of English-language culture, especially insofar as these involve engagement with contentious real-world issues.

3.3 Regulations for commencement of studies exam

The compulsory commencement of studies exam will be held before the end of September on the first semester of the Bachelor’s degree programme.
The exam generates 0 ECTS credits and is a pass/fail internal examination with no co-examiner. Two attempts to pass this exam are allowed. First-semester students will be registered for the exam automatically. The result will be posted in the student’s self-service system (mit.au.dk). Students who do not pass their first examination attempt will be registered automatically for their second attempt.
The result of the second attempt will be posted in the student’s self-service system (mit.au.dk) no later than 28 September. Students who fail both their first and second attempt will be withdrawn from their degree programme with immediate effect and without further warning.

3.4 Credit and flexibility

Students may apply for advance approval of degree programme elements which are subsequently completed at another university or institution of higher education in Denmark or abroad. An approved application for advance approval of transfer credit obligates the student to document the results – both passed and failed degree programme elements, as soon as these marks are available. The board of studies may – if necessary – procure the necessary documentation directly. The student will then receive transfer credit for the passed degree programme elements.

Applications for advance approval of transfer credit and submission of documentation for completed degree programme elements with a view to registration of transfer credit must take place in accordance with the rules published on the study portal. In addition, the board of studies may approve applications for transfer credit for degree programme elements which have been completed at an institution of higher education in Denmark or abroad.

When the board of studies has approved the credit transfer of a passed course/course element from an institution of higher education in Denmark or abroad, credit must be transferred as ‘Passed’. If the course element in question is assessed in accordance with the 7-point grading scale at both institutions, the assessment is transferred with the grade given, cf. the Degree Programme Order and the Examination Order.


3.5 Registration and withdrawal

Registering for compulsory courses, elective courses and exams
Registering for compulsory courses and elective courses:
Students will be registered automatically for teaching in compulsory courses in the order of semesters prescribed in the academic regulations. Students are responsible for ensuring that they are always registered correctly for courses and elective courses. Students must register for and (when necessary) prioritise elective courses by the set deadline, which can be found on the study portal. After this deadline, elective courses become a compulsory part of the student’s degree programme and cannot be changed.

Students who stick to the prescribed curriculum will be given priority when places are assigned for compulsory courses. After the deadline for registration, places can only be assigned to students if there is room in the classroom, and only until the start of the semester.

Selection criteria for elective courses
There may be a limit to the number of students admitted to elective courses, and the creation of such courses is contingent on the number of students who register for them. The criteria for allocating places on elective courses which only admit a limited number of students are stated on the study portal. (studerende.au.dk/arts).

Registering for and withdrawing from ordinary exams and re-examinations:
Students will be registered automatically for ordinary exams in compulsory courses. Students are responsible for ensuring that they are always registered correctly for exams. Students are responsible for registering for re-examinations by the set deadline, which can be found on the study portal and in the exam plan. Students can only register for re-examinations if they have already tried to pass the ordinary exam before the re-examination.

Students can withdraw from exams using the self-service system at mitstudie.au.dk, following the procedure described on the study portal. Students cannot withdraw from exams after the deadline, and the failure to take part in an exam for which they are registered will count as a failed attempt to pass it. The deadlines for registration and withdrawal can be found on the study portal and in the exam plan. Students cannot withdraw from the Master’s thesis.

Forms of exam for ordinary exams and re-examinations
The form of exam for ordinary exams and re-examinations is stated in the academic regulations. The ordinary form of exam is offered immediately after courses have been taught.
Students taking exams in the re-examination period will be examined using the form of re-examination that has been specified.

If the ordinary exam and the re-examination both use the same form of exam, students are entitled to apply for a re-examination in the ordinary exam period immediately after the course teaching has been completed. Deadlines are stated on the study portal. Students should not expect any requests submitted after the deadline to be granted.


3.6 Spelling and fluency

In the assessment of Bachelor’s projects and other major written assignments, emphasis must be placed on the student’s spelling and fluency in addition to the student's command of the content knowledge.

In the assessment of all written exams, emphasis will be placed on the extent to which the student is able to present an academic topic and structure an academic assignment including complying with formal academic requirements (references, quotations, etc.). In the assessment of all oral exams, emphasis is placed on the student's ability to present academic information, structure a presentation and take part in constructive academic dialogue.


3.7 Regulations for assignments

Conditions regarding the extent of written submissions are stated in the description of each degree programme component. A normal page for written submissions is 2400 characters (with spaces). To calculate normal pages, both text and notes are included, but not the front page, table of contents and bibliography. Written submissions that do not comply with these conditions cannot be accepted for assessment.

3.9 Project-oriented courses

Options for project-oriented courses are stated in the description of the individual study element. It is possible to take project placement with more than one project host if it is deemed to live up to all requirements for project placement.


3.10 Exemption

An exemption is a deviation from that or those regulations that normally apply for the area in question. Exemption can be granted on the basis of an application sent to the authority that has the power to grant such exemption. An application for exemption must be submitted to the Board of Studies. If another authority has the power to grant exemption, the Board of Studies forwards the application to the appropriate authority (e.g. the dean, rector or ministry). An application for exemption must be made in writing, stating reasons, and submitted as soon as possible. For the application to be processed immediately, it must include a precise account of the regulation from which exemption is sought, and what such exemption is intended to achieve (e.g. permission to use special aids, extension of examination time, postponement of time limits). Documentation for the unusual conditions that justify exemption must be enclosed with the application. Importance will not normally be attached to such conditions if they are not documented. More information about exemptions may be found on the study portal.


3.11 Appeals and complaints

Complaints about exams should be submitted to the university. For the application to be processed, it must be made in writing. The complaint must state both the reason for the complaint and what the complainant expects to achieve. Complaints about exams must be submitted within fourteen days after the deadline for the publication of exam results, cf. the Examination Order. More information about exam complaints is available on the study portal.

3.12 Examinations

Calculation of average grade point
It is possible to get the weighted average grade point calculated and formally verified. The weighted average grade point is an expression of the average grade point calculated in relation to the ECTS weighting of the grade points. The average grade point is calculated to one decimal point based on all graded exams. The average grade is not calculated in relation to the grades of fellow students, and that the calculation of the average mark is not offered until the programme's last exam has been passed.

3.13 Exchange

A degree programme may include study abroad. More information is available on the study portal.
